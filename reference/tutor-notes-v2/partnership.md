# Partnership

Status: draft. Original material for human review.

In a standard partnership problem, a stated distributable profit is
shared according to an agreed rule. When the rule is proportional to
capital and time, a partner's weight is capital invested multiplied
by the time it remains invested. This is a modelling assumption supplied
by the question, not a universal rule about business or legal entitlements.

If two partners invest capitals C1 and C2 for durations t1 and t2,
their profit shares have ratio C1t1:C2t2 under that rule. If the total
profit is P, the first share is PC1t1/(C1t1 + C2t2), and the second
uses C2t2 in the numerator. Use the same currency and time units for
all partners. Scaling both weights equally leaves the shares unchanged.

The capital-time product accounts for both size and duration. Equal
capitals invested for different times do not have equal weights, and
different capitals invested for the same time are weighted only by
their capital ratio. The denominator in each share includes every
partner's weight; it is not just the other partner's weight.

For example, one partner invests 6,000 rupees for twelve months and
another invests 9,000 rupees for eight months. Their weights are
72,000 and 72,000 rupee-months, so their shares are equal. If the
distributable profit is 5,000 rupees, each receives 2,500. Checking
the two shares gives the original total profit of 5,000.

When one partner changes capital, split the investment into intervals.
For example, 4,000 rupees for three months followed by 6,000 for
nine months gives a weight of 4,000 × 3 + 6,000 × 9 = 66,000
rupee-months. Do not multiply the final capital by the whole year.
Compare this total weight with the other partners' weights before
allocating the profit.

Common mistakes are ignoring joining dates, using capital alone when
durations differ, and allocating the same money twice when salary or
commission is mentioned. If a salary is explicitly paid before profit
sharing, subtract it before applying the ratio. These notes do not
cover changing ownership contracts, borrowing costs, tax treatment,
or a sharing rule that the problem has not specified.
