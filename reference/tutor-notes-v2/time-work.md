# Time and work

Status: draft. Original material for human review.

Work-rate problems compare a task with the rate at which it is
completed. Set the whole task to one unit when no physical quantity
is needed. If a worker completes it in a days at a constant rate,
the rate is 1/a task per day. In general, work = rate × time.
The rate, time and work units must be consistent.

For independent workers contributing to the same divisible task,
their rates add under the assumption that working together does
not change their individual efficiencies. If one takes a days and
another takes b days alone, their combined rate is 1/a + 1/b.
The time for one task is the reciprocal of that sum, namely
ab/(a + b), for positive a and b.

For example, one person finishes a job in twelve days and another
in eighteen days. Their rates sum to 1/12 + 1/18 = 3/36 + 2/36
= 5/36 of the job per day. Together they need 36/5 = 7.2 days.
This is less than either person's solo time, which is a useful
check when both contribute positive work throughout.

An alternative is to choose a convenient total number of work units.
For the same example, set the task to 36 units. The workers then
complete three and two units per day, so together they complete
five. Dividing 36 units by five units per day again gives 7.2 days.
The unit choice changes the numbers used, not the physical answer.

If work happens in stages, calculate the completed fraction in
each stage and subtract it from one. A worker at rate 1/12 for
three days completes 3/12 = 1/4 of the job. The remaining work
is 3/4. A second stage must use that remainder, not the original
whole task, when calculating how much longer the job will take.

Common mistakes are adding completion times instead of rates,
inverting only one term of a rate sum, and assuming twice as many
workers always halves time despite changed efficiency. State the
constant-rate and independent-contribution assumptions. These notes
do not cover alternating-day schedules, unequal worker categories,
or detailed pipe-and-leak conventions unless those methods are
provided separately.
