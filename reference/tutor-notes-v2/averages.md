# Averages

Status: draft. Original material for human review.

The arithmetic mean of n values is their sum divided by n, with n positive.
It represents the equal share if the total were redistributed among all
observations. If the mean is A, the total is nA. The mean retains the units
of the observations: the average of lengths in centimetres is also a
length in centimetres.

To combine groups, recover their totals first. If groups of sizes m and n
have means A and B, their combined mean is (mA + nB)/(m + n). This is a
weighted mean. The group sizes are the weights because every observation
counts once. The unweighted average (A + B)/2 works only when the groups
have equal sizes, or when the problem deliberately gives each group equal
weight rather than each observation.

For example, four students have an average score of 60 and six students
have an average score of 70. Their totals are 4 × 60 = 240 and 6 × 70 =
420. The combined total is 660 across ten students, so the mean is 66.
The answer lies between 60 and 70 and is closer to 70 because that group
contains more students.

When one observation changes, use the change in the total. Replacing x
by y among n observations changes the mean by (y − x)/n. If a new
observation joins, both the total and the count change. For example, five
values with mean 12 total 60. Adding a value of 18 gives total 78 over six
values, so the new mean is 13.

Adding a constant c to every observation adds c to the mean. Multiplying
every observation by a constant k multiplies the mean by k. These rules
follow by applying the operation to the total and dividing by the same
number of observations. They do not justify averaging ratios with different
denominators or averaging speeds without considering travel time.

Common mistakes are averaging group means equally, changing the total
without changing the count after an addition, and confusing the mean with
the middle value. A single extreme observation can strongly affect a mean.
These notes cover arithmetic and weighted means, not medians, modes,
variance, or general average-speed problems across unequal journeys.
