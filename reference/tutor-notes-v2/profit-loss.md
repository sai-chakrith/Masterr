# Profit and loss

Status: draft. Original material for human review.

Cost price, C, is the amount paid to obtain an item under the question's
definition. Selling price, S, is the amount received when selling it.
Profit is S − C when S exceeds C. Loss is C − S when C exceeds S.
Use consistent currency units and include additional expenses only when
the question explicitly makes them part of the cost.

Under the usual aptitude convention, profit percentage is 100(S − C)/C
and loss percentage is 100(C − S)/C, with C positive. The denominator
is the cost price. Profit margin uses selling price as its denominator
and is a different measure. Read the wording rather than treating all
percentages associated with a sale as interchangeable.

A profit of p% gives S = C(1 + p/100). A loss of l% gives S = C(1 − l/100).
These are percentage-change multipliers. To recover an unknown cost from
a sale price and profit percentage, divide the sale price by 1 + p/100.
Subtracting p% of the selling price uses the wrong base and usually gives
a different value.

For example, an item costs 500 rupees and sells for 600 rupees. Profit is
600 − 500 = 100 rupees. Profit percentage is 100 × 100/500 = 20%.
The multiplier check gives 500 × 1.20 = 600. The same 100 rupees is
100/600 of the selling price, so the margin is not 20%.

A marked price M is a quoted price before a discount. A discount d% gives
S = M(1 − d/100). For example, marking an item at 800 rupees and offering
a 25% discount gives S = 800 × 0.75 = 600 rupees. If its cost is 500,
the profit remains 100 rupees, or 20% of cost. Marked price is not cost
price unless the question states that they coincide.

Common mistakes are using selling price as the profit-percentage base,
adding successive discounts, and assuming equal profit and loss
percentages cancel across different items. Compute actual costs and
receipts before combining transactions. These notes do not provide a
method for tax rules, depreciation, credit charges, or deceptive weights.
Those require additional stated assumptions and topic material.
