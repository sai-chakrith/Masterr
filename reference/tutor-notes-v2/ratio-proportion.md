# Ratio and proportion

Status: draft. Original material for human review.

A ratio compares quantities measured in compatible units. The ratio a:b
means that for every a parts of the first quantity there are b parts of
the second. Multiplying or dividing both terms by the same nonzero factor
does not change the ratio. Convert units before comparing lengths, masses
or times; 1 metre to 50 centimetres is 100:50, or 2:1.

If two quantities are in the ratio a:b, write them as ak and bk for a
common scale k. Their total is (a + b)k. This representation connects a
comparison to actual values. If a total T is divided in that ratio with
positive a and b, the shares are Ta/(a + b) and Tb/(a + b). The denominator
counts every part in the total, not only the other person's parts.

A proportion states that two ratios are equal. For nonzero denominators,
a/b = c/d implies ad = bc by multiplying both sides by bd. This cross-product
rule is an algebraic convenience, not permission to ignore what each
quantity represents. Keep corresponding quantities in matching positions.

For example, divide 240 rupees in the ratio 3:5. There are 3 + 5 = 8
parts, so one part is 240/8 = 30 rupees. The shares are 3 × 30 = 90 and
5 × 30 = 150 rupees. Check their sum, 90 + 150 = 240, and their ratio,
90:150 = 3:5. Dividing 240 by 3 or 5 alone would not find the common part.

Direct proportion means one quantity equals a constant times another.
At a fixed unit price, doubling the number of identical items doubles
their total cost. Inverse proportion means the product is constant; for
a fixed distance and positive constant speed, travel time equals distance
divided by speed. These relationships require the stated conditions to
remain fixed.

Common mistakes are reversing one ratio, mixing units, and assuming every
relationship is direct proportion. Identify the constant quantity before
choosing direct or inverse proportion. Ratios of averages cannot generally
be combined without their group sizes. These notes cover simple positive
ratios and proportional relationships, not multi-stage partnership or
mixture problems unless the required additional methods are supplied.
