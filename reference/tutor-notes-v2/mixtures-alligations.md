# Mixtures and alligation

Status: draft. Original material for human review.

A mixture combines quantities with different concentrations or unit
values. For a concentration problem, track both the total mixture and
the amount of the constituent of interest. If volume V has fraction c
of a constituent, its constituent amount is Vc. Express all volumes
in the same units and concentrations on the same scale before adding.

When volumes are assumed additive, mixing amounts V1 and V2 with
fractions c1 and c2 gives concentration
c = (V1c1 + V2c2)/(V1 + V2). This is a weighted mean because the
constituent amounts add. It is not usually the arithmetic mean of the
two concentrations. The arithmetic mean works when the mixed amounts
are equal.

For example, mix two litres of a 20% solution with three litres of a
40% solution. The constituent amounts are 2 × 0.20 = 0.4 litres and
3 × 0.40 = 1.2 litres. The total constituent is 1.6 litres in five
litres of mixture, so the concentration is 1.6/5 = 0.32, or 32%.
The result is closer to 40% because more of that solution was used.

Alligation rearranges the weighted-mean equation. If c1 < c < c2,
the amounts needed satisfy V1:V2 = (c2 − c):(c − c1). The lower
concentration gets the difference on the opposite side. This follows
from V1(c − c1) = V2(c2 − c), obtained by multiplying the mean
equation by V1 + V2 and collecting terms.

To make a 30% mixture from 20% and 50% solutions, use the ratio
(50 − 30):(30 − 20) = 20:10 = 2:1. Mixing two litres and one litre
gives (2 × 20 + 1 × 50)/3 = 30 percent. An attainable target must
lie between the two starting concentrations when both amounts are
positive; a target outside that range signals an inconsistent setup.

Common mistakes are averaging concentrations without weights, reversing
the alligation ratio, and applying volume formulas when the stated
concentrations are by mass. Identify the basis first. These notes
assume additive quantities and no chemical reaction or loss. Repeated
withdrawal-and-replacement problems require an additional method and
are not covered here.
