# Percentages

Status: draft. Original material for human review.

A percentage expresses a quantity per hundred of a specified base. Saying that
30% of a class travels by bus means the fraction 30/100 of the whole class.
Identify the whole before calculating: 20% of a small quantity need not exceed
10% of a larger quantity.

Convert p% to the fraction p/100 before multiplying. Thus p% of B is B × p/100.
If a part A and a whole B are known, A is 100A/B percent of B, provided B is not
zero. To recover the whole from a known percentage and part, use B = 100A/p,
where p is not zero. Keep the units of the original quantity in the answer.

A percentage increase uses the starting quantity as its base. If a value goes
from B to F, the percentage change is 100(F − B)/B for positive B. A negative
result means a decrease. Equivalently, an increase of p% multiplies B by
1 + p/100, and a decrease multiplies it by 1 − p/100. These multipliers help
keep the original base and the changed quantity distinct.

Successive percentage changes apply to successive bases, so multiply their
factors rather than adding their percentages. Increasing 200 by 10% gives
200 × 1.10 = 220. Decreasing that new value by 10% gives 220 × 0.90 = 198.
The net change is −2 from the original 200, or a 1% decrease. Equal increases
and decreases do not cancel because their bases differ.

For a reverse example, a price after a 20% discount is 640 rupees. The final
price is 80% of the original price P, so 0.80P = 640. Dividing gives P = 800
rupees. Check by computing the discount: 20% of 800 is 160, and 800 − 160 =
640. Adding 20% to 640 would give 768 and would use the wrong base.

Distinguish a percentage-point difference from a relative percentage change.
If a pass rate rises from 40% to 50%, it rises by 10 percentage points. Relative
to the old rate, it rises by 100 × (50 − 40)/40 = 25%. State which measure the
question asks for. Retain exact fractions or sufficient decimal places until
the last step; avoid rounding each multiplier separately.

These notes assume ordinary positive bases for growth and reduction questions.
They cover parts, wholes, changes and reverse changes. They do not provide a
method for probability, percentage errors with signed reference values, or
annualised growth across unequal time periods. If such a method is needed,
the notes do not cover it.
