# Simple interest

Status: draft. Original material for human review.

Simple interest is calculated on the original principal throughout the
specified period. Let P be the principal, r the rate expressed as a
percentage per year, and t the time in years. Interest I = Prt/100, and
the final amount A = P + I. Interest and principal have the same currency
unit. The rate and time must use compatible periods.

The formula follows from applying r/100 of the principal in each year.
For t years at a constant rate, that yearly interest is multiplied by t.
Previously earned interest is not added to the base for later interest.
This is the defining difference between simple and compound interest.
Do not choose simple interest solely because a question uses small numbers.

For a rate per year, six months is half a year and three months is a
quarter of a year. Convert the duration before substitution. If a problem
uses a stated day-count convention, apply that convention; these notes
do not assume that all financial contracts use the same number of days
per year. An annual percentage must not be multiplied directly by months.

For example, invest 2,000 rupees at 6% simple interest per year for
18 months. The time is 18/12 = 1.5 years. Interest is
2,000 × 6 × 1.5/100 = 180 rupees, and the amount is 2,180 rupees.
As a check, one year earns 120 rupees and the additional half-year earns
60 rupees, giving the same total interest.

To recover a missing principal, use P = 100I/(rt) when the rate and time
are nonzero. To recover a rate, use r = 100I/(Pt); to recover time, use
t = 100I/(Pr). Identify whether the supplied money figure is the interest
alone or the final amount. If it is the amount, first subtract the
principal when the principal is known.

Common mistakes are reporting the amount as interest, leaving time in
months, and compounding a balance in a simple-interest problem. Check
the size of the result against the annual interest before rounding.
These notes cover a fixed principal and fixed simple rate, not repayments,
changing rates, continuous accrual conventions, or compound interest.
